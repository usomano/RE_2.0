Babe, We Got Gnomes in Our Computer!

Alessandro Y. Longo

Drop #42 · December 15, 2025 · 23 min read

A conversation with Kinked

Hey everyone (▰˘◡˘▰)

Welcome back to this space. Drop #42 is a dialogue between me, Alessandro, and Frank (aka Kinked). Frank has been part of various REINCANTAMENTO projects - soundtracking Harvesting the Net and FORMICAIO, directing the video for PROGRAMMA 101.

So when Riforma released GN by 乃o乃 (Bob) and Ougdol - launching their FFT_Materialism series - we decided to publish this dialogue to explore the galaxy of Kinked and Riforma, one of the most ambitious and exquisitely bizarre world-building projects in the global underground. They call it New Weird Italia, for a reason, after all.

Beyond our usual focus, I’m glad this newsletter can make room for dialogues like this, opening space for artistic universes that intersect with ours, contaminating and enriching our practice through ongoing exchange.

Kinked is a sonic project dropping innerfield-ritualisms through fft_transitions, vocal gestures, and experiments with loudness, encouraging errors and intuition in approaching sound compositions. Is interested in tech-enchantments and sound ecologies. Also known as Lapo Sorride and Dori Sorride, Kinked is 1/3 of the label Riforma and the founder of the New Weird Italia and Misto Mame. Curates the radio show Hypersigil✨, for Radio Sygma. Find their music here and here.

I met Frank (aka Kinked) on a surprisingly sunny December morning in a café in Neukölln. As global warming milds even the notorious harshness of Berlin’s winter, we find a seat outside a great Turkish bakery on the canal to chat over a cappuccino, trying to absorb as much Vitamin D as possible while we talk.

The occasion is the release of Riforma’s latest release, a label co-founded by Frank, NOT399093, and Adriano Cava. Riforma, based between Turin and Berlin, is dedicated to experimental music, or as they define it on their page, “glidecore, screwgaze, blisscutters, derives of musique concrete and tape-ballardism”.

The album is “GN” by 乃o乃 and Ougdol, part of a new series called FFT_Materialism. We take the chance to converse about the music and the new series, Riforma’s approach to electronic music, using sound as an exploration tool, gnomes, and much more

AYL: I’m happy we finally found the time for this conversation. We’re talking with Frank from Riforma on the occasion of the release of “GN” by 乃o乃 (Bob) and Ougdol, part of a new series called FFT_Materialism.

I’ll start with an observation. As always, I’m really struck by the world-building around every Riforma release - it’s so rich and generous conceptually. It’s intriguing and abstract, but also playful in a way. There’s a certain irony to it, I think.

Tell me about the concept of the FFT_Materialism series.

Frank: This is our second attempt at creating a series. The first one was done by Adri [Adriano Cava, co-founder of Riforma and musician] a while ago (with the Liminal Fields series, which released the Roman duo Acchiappashpirt with a small publication and tape). Still, we didn’t have time to develop it properly.

This idea of creating a standalone story has always been in the air, different, for example, from a type of release we did years ago called “Batch#”, which also featured Elif [Elif Gülin Soğuksu, musician], reworking Giulia’s track [Giulia Rae, musician], or Kinlaw&Franco Franco.

I wouldn’t define “Batch#” as a series - they came out more or less annually: four releases, four tracks per release, without a real concept. It was a way to put together four tracks with possibly one or two unreleased and at least two or three reworks (not to be confused with remixes) [laughs].

This series, FFT_Materialism, follows a concept instead, developed over the course of a year or so, with a much more precise focus on an event that, in this case, is technical, theoretical, and aesthetic.

AYL: Yes, FFT is the name of an algorithm, right? FFT stands for Fast Fourier Transform.

Frank: Exactly! It’s an algorithm that was first theorized around the beginning of the 19th century and then developed in the 1960s or 70s.

By the way, one of the two inventors [Frank is referring to James Cooley and John Tukey, authors of the first official paper on FFT] ended up working for IBM, and from then on, with the explosion of the personal computer, this algorithm became the foundational basis of a series of processes now totally embedded in the use of digital technologies.

AYL: So, an algorithm that doesn’t originate in the musical context. Can you try to describe in a few words what the algorithm does?

Frank: I’ll try, though the technical side is still pretty obscure to me. I understand a lot of those terms because when you work with code or programs that use it, at some point, you figure out what to tweak to make things happen. But what’s actually behind it is even more obscure - it’s mathematics [laughs].

AYL: Right, so it’s more empirical, hands-on. You know how to use it and what it does to the sound.

Frank: For example, it’s used for analyzing signals in general - in this case, audio signals - and also for reconstructing them. It’s basically the foundation of any kind of digital communication, like a Skype call. At some point, since it’s an analysis tool and so much of computer music is based on signal analysis, it started being used aesthetically too, breaking through that barrier. Similar to a lot of ideas from mathematics, it became an aesthetic tool - it could be aestheticized, becoming a musical instrument in its own right.

AYL: So, this series is more your curation?

Frank: Yeah, yeah, it’s my curation - this technique is fundamental to my sound practice. I started tagging my releases as “FFT_Materialism” as a genre, one of the many styles we’ve invented. But it’s also very fitting, I think, like Tenco-Grime. At some point, I sensed there was a specific vibe growing around this kind of interaction with instruments that use these algorithms.

I noticed there were mainly two approaches: the more academic one, which uses and studies the analysis tool to achieve certain research goals, and then the purely aesthetic one. But as I wrote in the series introduction, the latter tends to be more “glamified” - FFT as a tool for romanticizing sound. When you use these algorithms, they mostly show up as a phase vocoder, which lets you change the timbre of a sound to get something more melodic or harmonic.

I don’t know, autotune or the classic vocoder you hear in tons of releases, especially here in Berlin - this very autotune-core style with these stretched-out, spacious sounds that feel almost aquatic.

AYL: Using autotune more spatially, rather than just correctively.

Frank: Exactly, a smoother use. The algorithm gets used in this glam way, this cute way, you know?

AYL: Right. You actually mention the whole cute thing in the record’s description, too, don’t you?

Frank: Yeah. So I wanted to create a third way, let’s say. Not necessarily strictly un-cute, but something that leaves room for thinking about the material itself - the algorithm’s materiality. With FFT Materialism, in the proper sense, it’s like: if you strip away the cosmetic function - the smoothing, the dilating of sound - what’s left? What can we actually contemplate? That’s what I mean by materialism here - exploring that materiality.

AYL: Very interesting. I noticed a passage in the series’ description that says: “an investigation which dresses the shapes of experimental music.” I wanted to ask how you see this - both in this release and more generally with Riforma and your musical practice. Using experimental music, the release format, and the tracks themselves as an actual research tool. It feels like there’s a much more internal approach to the technique you’re exploring, compared to using it from a distance, in a “laboratory” setting. There’s complicity here - you’re inside it, you’re people who actually use these tools, who know how they work.

How does the release relate to research for you? How do they connect? Is the release a way to present research findings, or is it more about encapsulating the research itself?

Frank: For Riforma specifically, we’re not academic people at all. The spirit has always been to keep ourselves free from the pressure of having to produce something that works in a research environment. That’s definitely not the goal.

When I talk about academic approaches with FFT, it’s because many people developing these things do come from academic backgrounds. But some people work empirically - like Asmus Tietchens, for example, who doesn’t come from academia at all. I love the way he describes his practice on his website: “self-taught appropriation of creative skills and handling of analogue and digital studio technology”. That’s very inspiring, because it shows another way of engaging empirically with this stuff. Honestly, in the series, many people come from academia - see Andreas, Elif, Lisa, Örlygur. 乃o乃 and I don’t. I don’t think Ogdol does either.

But the goal is definitely to create an aesthetic product that generates ideas beyond the single release. Each release gets enriched with other materials - like the EMST radio show series we curate on Radio Syg.Ma [Riforma curates Emerging Spectral Topographies, an extended webcasting edition of the series fft_Materialism] - and at the end of this whole series, we’ll probably condense all the ideas from each project into a small publication. Not with the expectation of contributing to academic research, but to give the series another kind of output and a certain historical legitimacy.

I really believe in condensing an experience into print. Beyond just liking the physical object, I think it gives a different kind of historicity - it imprints a permanence on physical media compared to digital, which by now feels more as a fluid extension rather than anything concrete.

We’re already doing it in a way: bringing in people who’ve finished their studies and then engaging with different worlds, including ours.

The GN release, for example, I think, is historically important because it exists in this underground context of an Italian label, but it’s a composition that could easily be computer music in academia. It’s interesting to see it completely decontextualized like that. 乃o乃 and Ogdol are people who were involved with Amour&Discipline [webzine on free culture] and Grrrnd Zero, which is a self-managed space.

AYL: Where?

Frank: In Lyon. Their experiences are linked to DIY, to experiences of decontextualization, of détournement. Experiences that have nothing to do with the rigidity of research. The goal is to enrich our network of friendships, of piatabene [Italian slang from pigliarsi bene - people who click, who get along well], and also to play with academic terms, reappropriating them.

AYL: Exactly. What’s interesting to me is the clear focus on a process, on an algorithmic technique, and its material effects on sound. In a way, this works as a research focus. Especially compared to what you were saying earlier about Batch#...

Someone comes up asking for a smoke - the first of four times this will happen while we talk. Frank hands one over without hesitation.

AYL: So, we were saying... there’s a really clear curatorial vision here. All the FFT Materialism releases use this algorithm as their aesthetic core, while exploring how it shapes sound’s materiality. Compared to Batch# or most label series that move by association - scenes, geographies, trends - this is a much stronger thematic focus. I find it fascinating.

Let’s talk about the actual release. How did this collaboration come about? How did you approach it? Was the FFT theme there from the start, or was it more something you proposed to them? And why did this become the first in the series? How do you see it opening things up for what comes after?

Frank: 乃o乃 and Ogdol reached out to me - I think early 2025, maybe late 2024. They emailed about a graphic collaboration, working with my Lapo Sorride alias for the release. When they sent me the music, I freaked out. I was like, “No way, this is it, this is happening!” I’d already been planning to make this whole thing concrete, so...

AYL: You found the FFT Materialism you were looking for.

Frank: Exactly! Originally, I was going to start the series with my own work, but I immediately jumped on this and proposed the concept to them. 乃o乃 wrote back, saying FFT_Materialism was exactly the phrase that captured what they’d been looking for. From there, it was a year of working on the release. That’s how it became the first one, and I think it fits perfectly.

I unpack the physical release: beyond the tape itself, there’s a series of printed materials, all centered around the lore of the release and its gnome world.

Frank: What you’re holding is basically a glossary of terms. There’s an important work on the imaginary here. They’ve got their own trip on this theme of gnomicity.

AYL: Right, that’s what I wanted to understand.

Frank: They have an autonomous trip with this whole gnome thing. 乃o乃 and Ougdol describe an environment and a journey through it. It could even be seen as a kind of hero’s journey, which is why they created this series of maps and a textual guide - a glossary with terms related to their conception of gnomics. That’s how this whole world emerged. These are plurimaps - they can all function as maps - and then there are cards you can use to develop your own narrative around the journey, the exploration.

AYL: Incredible. I wanted to talk about lore precisely. I think it’s a characteristic of the riformese style and what I’d be curious to understand...

Frank: It’s nice that you said riformese and not riformista [laughs].

AYL: Riformista seemed a bit moderate to me [laughs].

So, we were saying... I see Riforma making this really successful attempt to play with narratives, with terms, even with the platform itself, where you publish. It’s usually Bandcamp, right? Where you can tag releases and map out your own genres. And you use that as an expressive tool - Tenco-Grime, Screw-Gaze, all these semantic inventions.

I’ve noticed that a lot of musicians have this difficulty - even hostility - around genre labels. Whether they come from critics or the internet, in whatever form. There’s often this dismissive, almost populist attitude toward what are basically just terms for understanding each other, for talking about what we’re hearing, for connecting different sonic expressions.

How do you feel about that? Do you think that’s real in the scene? Because Riforma’s approach feels much more playful, way less hostile. It almost seems like you’re saying, “Okay, there’s this whole game of micro-genres, hyper-specific tags in the data sea? Fine, we’ll make our own, we’ll define ourselves.” Tell me if I’m reading that right.

Frank: Look, first thing about genres: they don’t exist. I mean, they exist as market categories, but the styles themselves are super fluid. So I get people who reject these labels. If you ask me what genre I make, I don’t give you a genre - I explain what I do in terms of aesthetic ideas. And I do that because I’ve noticed it actually works; people understand when you frame it that way.

This whole playing with genres and styles is something we’ve always done - not just in music, but aesthetically in general. It comes from spending time at places like Fanfulla [Fanfulla 5/a in Rome], where I grew up culturally. You’d see these wild posters with genre names that riff on the main genre but spin off in totally playful directions.

That’s where it comes from - going to Fanfulla, seeing all these worlds collide, and at some point you just start inventing genres yourself. It makes sense, it helps convince even yourself that you’re creating that aesthetic. And there’s this pleasure in the wordplay itself, the fantasy it builds. We’ve always had that with Misto Mame, with New Weird Italia - it all starts from wordplay or just spontaneous flashes, without overthinking it [Misto Mame is the eclectic collective Frank founded in the omonimous apartment in Rome in 2015].

Tenco-Grime is Tenco-Grime, you know? There’s no theory behind it. But once you start using the term, you realize, fuck, Dori Sorride [one of Frank’s aliases] actually IS Tenco-Grime! Because it’s not real grime, but it’s this attempt at autotuned grimeification, slurred spoken word, rooted in the suffering of love, the pain of being unhomed, to use Brandon Labelle’s term - never feeling at home anywhere. That’s pure Tenco - that’s the Tenco part, obviously not the grime part.

AYL: I love Dori’s record precisely for its Tenco soul.

Frank: And regarding Bandcamp, I actually find it very interesting to use tags - because that’s what they are in the end, not genres but tagging systems, right?

AYL: Yes, yes.

Frank: You can also tag something random like Cosenza. Any tagging system is filtering, which creates a database. What’s interesting is you’re essentially using Bandcamp as a database - when you tag something FFT_Materialism, you’ve got the whole FFT_Materialism catalog right there.

Other people who feel connected to that style don’t even need to release on Riforma itself, because the Bandcamp tag is there. You click it and boom, there are all those releases. It’s a way to hack Bandcamp, basically - creating these shadow catalogs that only exist if you click the tag. Otherwise, you’d never see them. It’s a discovery system too. Personally, when I want to find new stuff, I just type random tags in the search bar.

AYL: Right, what I find interesting is exactly that - taking control of it. The whole discourse around algorithmic music curation goes the opposite way, where for commercial reasons - whether you’re Drake or Oneohtrix Point Never - you get dumped into the ambient playlist so whoever listens to that playlist discovers you.

I see it as an autonomous way of using tags as an indexing tool on digital platforms. Creating your own micro-niche, your own micro-database, but also pulling other people in. Building scenes, mostly online. I don’t know if you like the word “scene,” but... They’re aggregators, right? They show who can find themselves in a certain vibe.

Frank: Exactly. Some kid wants to make Tenco-Grime, they do it, and they’re automatically connected to us through that term - even if we’ve never talked.

AYL: Let’s go back to GN and its world. What I find fascinating is how present the materiality of sound is - very rarefied but with these spooky tendencies, sounds appearing and disappearing. Track 6, track 9 - without reading “gnomes,” you’d think ghosts and a haunted atmosphere.

But then there’s this very clear gnome imaginary. The gnome as this figure trapped in the computer, in the digital realm.

Where does this come from? Gnomes in online aesthetics are usually tied to fantasy, cottagecore, and Dungeon Synth. Here, it’s describing a different sonic landscape. And when you give such a specific imaginary - how does that shape listening? The listener might hear ghosts, then discover they’re gnomes. How much does that guide versus limit the imagination?

Frank: I think the gnome thing - I don’t know exactly what their specific trip is with it - but to me it evokes something way more material than spectral. Even though FFT suggests “spectral,” and your first instinct might be something ethereal, airy, auric.

But I think that’s the game here. In the release, there are sounds with this dimensionality - literally in terms of size - that evoke a miniaturization of experience. In the processes they used, too - stretching certain sounds, isolating harmonics or transients - there you find the micro-materiality.

The gnome connects to exploring mineral worlds, making contact with the materiality of a cave, the underground. The gnome is a presence, as the physical capacity to navigate that kind of environment. That’s the link I see.

About how evocative it should be: I agree about leaving space open, but I don’t mind providing some guidelines either. The release could’ve worked both ways: leaving imagination completely open, or giving it a starting point like the gnomic setting.

The spectrality you picked up on - that’s exactly the materiality I’m talking about, exactly how it manifests. It’s interesting how they work with that. Each release in the series has its own complete lore, its own definition of what’s happening.

AYL: I’m really fascinated by this connection between sound materiality and algorithmic processes. I was just looking at this paper by Shintaro Miyazaki, a media studies professor here at Humboldt, titled ‘Algorhythmics: Understanding Micro-Temporality in Computational Cultures‘. He talks about how in the ‘50s, engineers would listen to computers to diagnose problems - they used listening as a debugging method.

And that’s what I see here - you’ve taught me to notice this - attention to the materiality of sound. Understanding a texture, an evolution, a process, as exploring sonic matter itself.

So it makes sense that there are gnomes and not ghosts [laughs]. You’re listening to an FFT algorithm, and you find a gnome inhabiting the computer, not a ghost. That is the materialistic approach. What other creatures can we expect in the world of FFT_Materialism?

Frank: I can talk about my release, which will be the next one.

Although I’ve been using these processes for a while, I decided to start from scratch. I have material from live sets, but lately I’ve been exploring new software, specifically Pure Data. My process is way more tied to memory: things to remove or let emerge. Memory is a specter in that sense, particles hanging in the air. The memory of matter, as seen by an algorithm, interpreted through it, materialized by it.

The release will be seven or eight tracks, starting from improvisations with these patches. I put everything together using a spectral editor, as I don’t use multitrack DAWs like Ableton. It’s an unusual approach, but not that different from how people composed in the past, literally joining tapes or recording sequential takes. The difference is it’s on the spectrum, so you get this very specific, precise intervention on what happens in the sound. That’s what interests me about spectral editing, both conceptually and materially.

It revolves around memories I have trouble remembering. I don’t remember many things - or better, I know my memories exist, but I don’t really remember them. In this release, they take the form of binaural field recordings: me walking, contemplating, improvising in the environment with the laptop, but recording everything binaurally in the field where I’m improvising.

When I discovered field recording, I started having a different relationship with the memory of those events - those walks, those contemplations. Treating that material evokes those memories and lets me explore them deeper. Processing through FFT algorithms is looking through the machine’s eyes, relating with it to extract other material, to expand that memory.

It’s very dynamic, like my style, with just a few static moments, very animated. Nothing synthetic - no synths, no drums. Everything comes from the organicity of field recordings and FFT processing that creates substantial, interesting rhythmic dynamics.

There’s a contemplative dimension too. If you stop to listen to the materiality of the sound object rather than just summing up the aesthetic experience, you can get really interesting sensations. These things have this fascinating materiality of their own. As I said before about online calls, just imagine a Zoom call with its glitches. If you focus not on the words, not on reconstructing what the person is saying, but on the quality of the distortions, the glitches themselves, you start feeling pleasure in that listening experience. Maybe this whole series comes from that - the pervasiveness of these glitchy dynamics you experience through digital communications, Zoom, WhatsApp, whatever.

Another future release by Elif, for example, is much more about overhearing: voices from things, things you shouldn’t hear but do. Things that have physicality, that take on vocal presence.

AYL: I sense a peculiar attention to digitality here.

Frank: This stuff is fucking digital.

AYL: Exactly, but with one keyword: awareness. An awareness that’s also attention - it reminds me of contemplative practices, like deep listening: processes that shift your focus to what’s around you. Recognizing that a voice processing technique on Zoom creates a specific timbre. We’re talking about timbre itself, so it’s attention from the inside rather than the outside. The materiality of the tool - not just what it does, returning your voice so we can communicate, but how it does it, what it affects in the process.

One last thing I wanted to ask: beyond your exploration of these themes, how do you see this relating to the bigger questions around music and algorithms? The pervasiveness of the digital, its impact on composition and performance.

Frank: If I understand your question right, I think there’s this widespread attraction toward digital materiality. If you think about the ‘70s, the synthesizer immediately became Kosmik - a tool to reach outer space, the Solar System, and beyond. Now I think there’s much more materialism, recognizing yourself as living in a certain materiality. It’s no longer looking beyond; it’s looking inside. The digital gets contextualized in those terms.

AYL: Right, that reminds me of Kit Mackintosh’s book on autotune, Neon Screams. It talks about musical futurism and how all these English critics - Simon Reynolds and others - complain it doesn’t exist anymore. But the futurism of Kosmik Musik, of Kraftwerk, was tied to robots, to space, projecting outward. Now there’s this awareness that the digital is in us: intrinsically, pervasively, in everything we do.

Right now we’re talking, and there isn’t even a physical voice - we’re being recorded, we’ll be transcribed, and it all happens almost without thinking. This project feels like a way to actually think about it, to act on it, to make art from this awareness. If there’s a cyborg, it’s inside us - not as in transhumanism but just the simple recognition that all the tools we use do something to us. My voice through Zoom isn’t the same as my voice talking to you in person.

Frank: About materiality: FFT also speaks to the process behind it in ecological terms. What you can do with it depends on computing power. I was reading today about FFT evolutions, like Quantum FFT. The dynamics of any digital processing formula evolve with computing power. What does that mean? If you can process at certain numbers, it’s because you have the computational power to do it. My processing isn’t lo-fi: it’s the maximum I can achieve with the resources I have. A certain type of processing, audio quality, Zoom call quality also tells you what’s behind it: which processors, why someone has that processor versus another.

AYL: So FFT_Materialism isn’t just about the Fourier algorithm as abstract instructions, but also accounts for the material substrate where the computation happens.

Frank: Exactly. And FFT is so widespread that VST suites I’ve been using for over ten years use it, such as Spectral Magic by Michael Norris. God bless Michael Norris, really [laughs]. Norris condensed these processes into practical, immediate tools for making music. But they’re so powerful that just making music with them feels banal.

FFT’s spread also comes from the fact that one of its creators, John Tukey, didn’t work at IBM - unlike James Cooley - so he prevented the corporation from acquiring the patent. It stayed in the public domain. [laughs] This process was basically born alien, unwilling to be patented. It stayed in circulation.

AYL: And you’re reappropriating it.

Frank: Exactly! You have to know how to use what’s there.
